**Analysis & Step‑by‑step Reasoning**

1. **Identify the high‑level objective**  
   The mission is to ensure that food is delivered to an inpatient room *and* that any dirty dishes left behind are removed.  These are two distinct operational activities, both of which must succeed for the system to be considered functional.  Consequently the top‑level goal is an **AND** of “Deliver food” and “Retrieve dishes”, and we choose a *sequential* runtime because the robot is typically busy delivering the meal, so dish retrieval can only begin once the delivery is complete.

2. **Model the “Deliver food” branch**  
   - The robot can either **directly place the meal on the patient’s table** or **deposit it on its own tray** for later pickup by a patient, companion, nurse, or another robot.  This is an OR‑choice.  
   - However, the system should try the simpler, more direct option first; only if that fails (e.g., patient cannot reach the table, door closed, etc.) should the robot fall back to the tray method.  This is expressed with a **fallback** relation.  
   - The “direct” option decomposes into three atomic actions: approach the room, open the door (possibly with a human or another robot), and place the meal on the table.  
   - The “tray” option involves depositing the meal on the tray, checking whether a patient, companion, nurse, or another robot can retrieve it (a **query**), indicating which meal to pick up, waiting for the retrieval, tracking the event, and alerting the patient if the wrong meal is taken.

3. **Model the “Retrieve dishes” branch**  
   - Dish retrieval can proceed **unassisted**, with **robot cooperation**, or with **human assistance**.  These are mutually exclusive alternatives, so an OR decomposition is appropriate.  
   - As with food delivery, we prefer the simplest strategy first, so we use a **fallback chain**: try unassisted → robot cooperation → human assistance.  
   - Each strategy decomposes into atomic actions: approach the room, open the door (robot‑only, robot‑robot, or robot‑human), collect the dishes, and place them in the trash or confirm completion.

4. **Define query goals**  
   - The system must obtain the patient’s retrieval capability and the presence of a companion from the patient record.  This is a **Query** goal that returns the needed information before the tray‑delivery strategy can proceed.

5. **Task design**  
   - Every atomic action is expressed as a task.  
   - Tasks include the location (“hospital room”) and the number of robots required.  
   - If a task might need cooperation, we express it as a range, e.g., `[1,2]` robots.  
   - Tasks are linked to their parent goal with an **AND** relation because all of them must succeed for the goal to succeed.

6. **Goal and task numbering**  
   - Goals: depth‑first traversal gives `G1` → `G1.1` → `G1.1.1` → `G1.1.2` → `G1.1.2.1` → `G1.2` → `G1.2.1` → `G1.2.2` → `G1.2.3`.  
   - Tasks: depth‑first traversal gives `AT1` … `AT23`.  

7. **Runtime annotations**  
   - **Top‑level (`G1`)**: sequential `;` (food delivery must finish before dish retrieval starts).  
   - **`G1.1`**: fallback `FALLBACK(G1.1.1, G1.1.2)` (try direct delivery first).  
   - **`G1.2`**: nested fallback `FALLBACK(FALLBACK(G1.2.1, G1.2.2), G1.2.3)` (try unassisted first, then robot cooperation, finally human assistance).  

8. **Goal types**  
   - All leaf goals are **Perform** goals because they involve executing actions.  
   - `G1.1.2.1` is a **Query** goal.  
   - No **Achieve** goals are needed because we are not specifying a future world state beyond the completion of tasks.
